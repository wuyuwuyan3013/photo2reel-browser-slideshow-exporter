IMAGE TO VIDEO
Practical image to video guide

Write image to video motion prompts that keep the photo intact.

Describe only four things: the subject, one movement, one camera move, and one change in the scene. Then explicitly name what must remain unchanged.

The usable prompt pattern

[Subject] + [single action] + [camera movement] + [environment movement] + [preservation limits]. Keeping each slot narrow gives a video model less room to invent a different face, object, setting, or composition.

Start with a motion decision, not a style label

An image already supplies the scene. Repeating adjectives such as “cinematic,” “beautiful,” or “high quality” does not tell the model what should move. Decide what a viewer should notice first: a person looking up, a product rotating slightly, a curtain moving, or a camera pushing in. Keep the request to one main action for a short clip.

Portrait“She turns her head slightly toward the window; loose hair moves in a light breeze.”
Product“The bottle remains centered while the camera makes a slow 10-degree orbit; soft reflections shift.”
Landscape“Clouds drift left to right; the camera slowly pushes forward along the path.”
Illustration“The character blinks once and raises one hand; the background remains a still painted scene.”

Use the five-part prompt in this order

  1. Name the existing subject. Refer to the visible person, product, animal, or object rather than asking the model to introduce someone new.
  2. Choose one physical action. A nod, a look, one step, a small turn, or a hand movement is easier to preserve than a chain of actions.
  3. Choose one camera behavior. Use a slow push-in, gentle pan, locked camera, or subtle orbit. Do not ask for multiple cuts in a single short result.
  4. Give the scene one secondary movement. Wind in fabric, steam, light reflections, or distant traffic can make the frame feel alive without changing its subject.
  5. Add limits. State what must not change: identity, clothing, product label, background, composition, and camera framing.
Portrait prompt The person in the reference photo keeps the same face, hair, clothing, and background. She takes one small breath, looks toward the camera, and smiles softly. Locked medium shot with a very slow push-in. A light breeze moves only a few strands of hair. No face changes, no extra people, no new objects, no text.
Product prompt Keep the exact product shape, label, colors, and tabletop composition from the reference image. The camera makes a slow left-to-right orbit while soft studio reflections travel across the packaging. The product does not bend, open, rotate by itself, or change branding. No hands, no new items, no text overlay.

When the result drifts, remove choices before adding detail

If a face changes, a label mutates, or a new object appears, do not answer with a longer paragraph. Reduce the number of moving parts. Lock the camera, remove a secondary action, and state the preservation limit in plain language. A narrow prompt is a clearer instruction than a crowded one.

Face or identity changesAsk for “same face and identity from the reference” and limit the action to a blink, glance, or small head turn.
Product label changesAsk for a locked camera first; then add only lighting movement or a small camera push-in.
Background becomes new scenerySay “keep the existing background unchanged” and avoid words that imply a journey, cut, or location change.
Motion is too strongReplace “dynamic” or “dramatic” with an observable speed: slow, subtle, gentle, or one small movement.

Choose a frame that supports the movement

A vertical frame is useful when the subject is a person, a phone-first product shot, or a social clip. A wider frame gives a landscape, vehicle, or group photo room for a pan or push-in. Before generating, leave visual space in the direction the camera or subject will move; otherwise the animation can feel like it is immediately running into the edge of the image.

Turn the prompt into a repeatable workflow

Write the first version in one sentence, generate a short result, then change one slot at a time. If the movement works but the camera is distracting, keep the action and replace only the camera instruction. This preserves what was successful and makes it clear which instruction caused the change.

Prompts only matter once a model is generating the movement; if you just want the photos assembled in order, how to make photos into a video skips that step.
