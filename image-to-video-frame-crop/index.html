FRAME
Before you upload

Image to video: start from the frame you actually want.

The model does not look at your photo. It looks at a resized, re-cropped copy of it, made to fit a frame you never chose. Whatever fell outside that frame is not in the video, and no prompt brings it back. This is the step before every other step.

The short answer

Decide the output frame first, then crop your photo to it yourself. A portrait photo going into a landscape output gets cut by software that does not know what matters in your image — usually the middle band, which is often the least interesting part. Crop it to the target ratio before uploading and you keep the decision. Leave it and the software decides for you, silently, and you find out after you have paid for the render.

Two resamples happen before you see a single frame

This is the part that explains most "the result does not look like my photo" complaints, and almost nobody describes it.

Your file goes through at least two conversions. First it is scaled to whatever internal size the model works at — usually smaller than your original, sometimes much smaller. Then the output is written at the delivery frame you selected. Neither step is lossless, and neither one asks you what should be preserved.

This is why a sharp original can come back soft, and why a wide shot can come back as a close-up of a wall. Neither is the model misunderstanding you. Both are the frame being decided by something that is not you.

Pick the frame, then crop to it

Three delivery frames cover almost everything. The right starting move is different for each, and none of them is "upload as is".

Vertical, from a portrait photoThe easiest case, and the one people overthink. Crop only if the photo has dead space at top or bottom. Keep headroom if you plan to burn captions in later — that space will be covered.
Landscape, from a portrait photoThe hardest case. You are removing most of the height, so decide deliberately which band survives: the face, the product, or the horizon. The service's default will take the centre, which is rarely the best band.
Square, from anythingLeast loss, most flexibility. Crop tight around the subject rather than centring the whole frame, because a square forces a decision that a rectangle lets you avoid.
What not to doDo not upload a 3:2 photo and ask for 9:16 with "keep everything". It cannot. Something is being removed, and you are choosing not to be the one who decides.

Three ways a vertical clip fails before generation starts

These are composition failures, not model failures. They are all decided in the crop, and all three are cheap to avoid.

  1. The head is cut, or the feet are.Vertical frames are unforgiving. If the subject's head sits near the top edge of the source, the first crop clips it. Pull the crop down before uploading, and leave a margin.
  2. The caption zone is eaten.Most vertical platforms overlay text and controls near the bottom. If your subject sits there, it will be covered. Leave the bottom band empty on purpose.
  3. The subject sits dead centre in a tall frame.A centred subject in 9:16 leaves two dead bands. Move the crop so the subject occupies the upper third and the lower third carries the motion or the caption.

Why the result comes back blurry

This is one of the most common searches in this category, and the usual assumption — that the model is low quality — is often wrong. Blur has three causes, and only one of them is the model.

Cause one: the source was already softA screenshot of a photo, a re-saved image, a crop taken from a larger picture. If the detail is not in the file you uploaded, it cannot appear in the output. Check the file at 100% before blaming the service.
Cause two: the crop was enlargedTake a small region and stretch it to fill a frame and you have invented pixels. The model will faithfully animate the invented ones. Crop from the largest version of the photo you have, not from a thumbnail.
Cause three: the model input is smaller than your fileAs in the diagram above, the model works at its own internal size. A very large upload does not survive at full detail. This is the only case where the service is responsible.
What does not fix itAdding "4K, sharp, high detail" to the prompt. Sharpness is decided by pixels that exist, not by adjectives. A prompt cannot restore detail that the resample removed.

The practical test: open the file you are about to upload at 100%, in the crop you intend to use. If it looks soft there, it will look soft in the video. That check takes ten seconds and saves a render.

Sixty seconds before you upload

  1. Name the delivery frame.9:16, 16:9, or 1:1. Not "I will decide at export" — the export choice is what cuts your photo.
  2. Crop to that ratio yourself, in the largest version you have.Any editor will do, including the browser. The point is that you choose the band, not the service.
  3. Look at the crop at 100%.Soft now means soft later. Fix it by finding a better source, not by adding adjectives.
  4. Leave the caption band emptyif the clip is going anywhere that overlays text at the bottom.
  5. Then describe the motion.The prompt comes after the frame, never before it.

The order that saves renders

Frame, then crop, then check at 100%, then describe motion, then generate. Every step after the crop is reversible. The crop is the one decision you cannot undo after paying.

If you only take one thing from this page: the model is not looking at your photo. It is looking at a copy that already lost the argument about what matters. Win that argument yourself, before the upload button.
