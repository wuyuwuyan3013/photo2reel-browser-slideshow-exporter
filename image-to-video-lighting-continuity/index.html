Lighting continuity

Let the light move without turning afternoon into night.

The short answer

Use the source photo as the lighting baseline. Allow one visible light event, then compare the source, middle, and final frames against three anchors: shadow direction, a neutral bright patch, and a time-of-day cue. If the entire scene changes color or the cues imply a different hour, reject that render and narrow the light event.

This is a review method, not a promise that a prompt can lock a model's lighting.

Choose a light event smaller than a new scene

There are two different jobs: preserving the source lighting exactly, and allowing a small lighting event while the scene remains the same. This page is for the second job. A thin cloud crossing a window, or a reflection sliding over a product, can be described as one event. “Become cinematic at sunset” asks for a different time and often a different scene.

The input image already carries composition, colors, and lighting. Runway's Gen-4 prompting guide describes that starting role and recommends direct wording focused on motion. Use that as a reason to inspect the image first, not as a guarantee about any model's output.

Shadow directionPick one visible cast shadow. Its softness may change; its source-to-object direction should still make sense.
Bright referencePick a pale wall, shirt, or tabletop already in the frame. Compare whether it turns uniformly yellow, blue, or blown out.
Time cueUse the sky, window, practical lamp, or other visible cue that tells the viewer what part of the day this is.

Make a boundary card before generating

  1. State the allowed event. Say what changes and where: a passing cloud softens the tabletop light, or one reflection travels across the bottle.
  2. Name the protected cues. Record the existing shadow direction, bright reference, and daylight or lamp cue. If a cue is absent, use only the ones visible.
  3. Hold the camera job still. A fixed viewpoint makes a lighting change easier to judge; a simultaneous pan changes what surfaces can be compared.
  4. Write the rejection rule now. Reject a clip if the bright reference shifts globally, the shadow reverses without a source change, or the final frame reads as another time of day.
Example for a window-lit tabletopA thin cloud passes the window and briefly softens the light on the tabletop. The window remains the daylight source, the tabletop shadow keeps its direction, and the camera stays locked.

This example describes an intended visual result. It does not claim that every generator will obey all three conditions. If the clip fails, change one part of the request or improve the starting image; do not call an unwanted relight a successful variation.

Review three frames, not just the thumbnail

Pause at the beginning, the middle of the event, and the final frame. Compare the same bright patch and shadow in each. A light event can rise and fall while the location still reads as one continuous shot. A whole-frame color cast, a new sun direction, or a sky that becomes evening breaks the stated boundary even when the motion looks attractive.

Questions people ask

Why does the clip brighten immediately after the first frame?First check whether the change is local or whole-frame. If the whole scene glows, compare the bright reference against the source and retry with a smaller event or a clearer starting image. A longer adjective list alone is not a reliable fix.
Can a cloud pass without changing the time of day?That is a reasonable target for one test. Describe the cloud's effect on one visible surface, keep the existing daylight source, and judge the final frame against the original time cue.
What if the source has no clear shadow?Do not invent one. Use the bright reference and visible time cue; if neither exists, state the intended change more narrowly and review overall color against the source.
