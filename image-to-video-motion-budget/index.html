Prompt planning

Choose what moves before you ask an image to become a video.

A short prompt works better when it gives one element the job of moving and makes the rest of the frame reviewable as fixed reference.

The short answer

Make a three-line motion budget: name one moving element, name the details that must stay fixed, and name one visible result you will check in the last frame. If the camera, subject, lighting, and background all need separate changes, reduce the job before generating.

The motion budget is a planning card, not a magic prompt

Image-to-video systems begin with your image and then make the frames that follow. The input supplies composition, colors, subjects, and lighting; your text is most useful when it describes the motion job. That does not mean a short prompt guarantees a particular render. It means you have a smaller, clearer change to inspect.

Motion ownerThe single item that earns the right to change: steam, leaves, a hand, water, or one person.
Protected referenceThe details you need to remain recognisable: a label, face, horizon, framing, or product position.
Camera stateOne declared condition: locked, a small push-in, or a gentle pan—not several camera jobs at once.
Acceptance evidenceA visible final-frame check, such as “the label remains readable” or “the horizon stays level.”

Write the card before the prompt

  1. Circle the one thing that may change. If the answer is “everything,” choose the smallest action that communicates the idea.
  2. List what cannot be reinterpreted. Use visible nouns from the input frame: the bottle label, the face outline, the room edge, or the camera angle.
  3. Set one camera condition. A locked camera is a usable condition; a camera that pans, zooms, shakes, and tracks at once is several competing jobs.
  4. Decide the final-frame proof. Check the protected detail in the last frame before you decide the clip is usable.

Use positive, physical wording for the selected job

Runway’s Gen-4 prompting guide recommends direct positive phrasing, a high-quality input, and starting simply before adding detail. It gives “locked camera” as a concrete state in place of repeated negative instructions. Treat that as a workflow cue rather than a universal model promise: state the condition you want, run a small test, and inspect the result.

Keep the test separate from a rescue attempt

When a result fails, do not immediately add a second camera direction or a pile of exclusions. Keep the same source and change one part of the motion budget. The one-change test keeps the comparison meaningful; use the render review sheet to record whether the promised motion happened without changing protected detail.

Questions people ask

Can a camera move and a subject move together?They can, but start by making one the main job. If the first test is unstable, freeze one of them before adding more detail.
What if the source frame is already tight?Do not use the motion budget to request missing scenery. Read the crop-room guide first and reduce or redirect the move.
