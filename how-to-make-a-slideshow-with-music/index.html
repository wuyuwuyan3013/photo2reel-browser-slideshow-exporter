Slideshow with music guide

How to make a slideshow with music.

Adding music to photos is the same job as making a slideshow with music — the photos need a duration before a song means anything. This page walks the order that keeps the two in sync, and covers the case where the slideshow already exists.

The short answer

Set the song length first, then divide it by the number of photos. A three-minute track over 30 photos gives six seconds each; if the average drops under two seconds, cut photos instead of speeding the slideshow up.

How to make a slideshow with music from scratch

Work backwards from the song. A slideshow with music is two timelines that have to land on the same frame, so the track decides how much room each photo gets. Pick the song first, note its length, then divide by the number of photos you actually want to show.

3-minute track, 30 photosSix seconds each. Comfortable for landscapes and product shots.
3-minute track, 60 photosThree seconds each. Fast but still readable for people and events.
3-minute track, 120 photos1.5 seconds each. Too fast to look at anything; cut photos instead.
90-second track, 20 photos4.5 seconds each. Good for a single subject or a portrait sequence.

Once the average is set, give individual photos their own duration. A title card or a wide establishing shot earns more time than the fourth photo of the same beach. One duration for everything is what makes a sequence feel like a slideshow rather than a video.

How to make a video with pictures and music

The only real difference between a slideshow and a video made from pictures is what the frame does. A slideshow holds a photo still and then changes it. A video with pictures and music usually moves inside the frame as well, and that changes how long each photo can stay on screen.

Hold stillSafest. Each photo needs 3–6 seconds; the transitions do the work.
Slow zoom or panTwo seconds of movement per photo already reads as video. Longer starts to drift.
Cut on the beatPhotos can drop to 1–2 seconds if every cut lands on a bar, because the music carries the rhythm.
MixedHold the important photos, cut fast on the chorus. This is what most people actually want.

Pick one of those before you pick a song. A three-minute track cut on the beat holds roughly 90 photos; the same track with static photos holds about 30. The music does not decide the length — the frame does.

How to make photos into a video with music

You do not need footage. Photos carry a video as long as three things are true: the order tells a story, each photo stays long enough to be read, and the music lands somewhere. Everything else — templates, transitions, titles — is decoration on top of those three.

  1. Sort before you style. Put the photos in the order you would tell the story out loud, and delete the ones you are keeping only because you took them.
  2. Set a baseline duration, then adjust the exceptions. Four seconds is a reasonable default; the opening shot and the last shot get two extra.
  3. Add the track last. Drop it in, check that the last photo is still on screen when the song ends, and trim one or the other.

If the photos come from more than one device, check the orientation before you start. A vertical photo in a landscape frame is not stretched — it sits over a dark background — but a run of vertical shots in a horizontal video looks like a mistake even when it is not.

How to add music to a slideshow you already have

If the slideshow already exists as an exported video with no audio, adding a track means re-rendering, not patching. A browser cannot write a new audio stream into an existing MP4; the file has to be produced again with both tracks present. That is why most instructions for adding music to a finished slideshow end up back at the export step.

  1. Re-export from the photos. Go back to the image sequence, add the audio, and render again. Slower, but the timing stays exactly as you set it.
  2. Record the slideshow playing with the track. Play the sequence and capture the canvas together with the audio. Faster, but the result is a screen recording and the quality ceiling is lower.

Either way, keep the source photos and the original audio file. Re-rendering is cheap; hunting down a song you no longer have is not.

How to put music on a slideshow without an app

A browser can do the whole job: read the images and the audio file locally, draw each photo for its duration, mix the track at the volume you set, and record the result to MP4. Nothing is uploaded, so there is no account, no server-side file limit, and no queue.

FormatMP3, M4A and WAV play in every modern browser. A file the browser cannot decode fails quietly, so test one photo before rendering thirty.
VolumeDrop the music to roughly 20–30% when photos will sit under narration later. Full volume leaves no room for a voice.
LengthA track longer than the slideshow gets cut at the last frame. A shorter track leaves silence at the end — loop it or remove photos.
OwnershipA browser-generated tone is the safe option when you do not own the track and the slideshow is going somewhere public.

Keep the join points quiet

Most slideshows feel wrong at the transition, not at the photo. Two seconds of cross-fade on a three-second photo means the image is never fully visible. If the track has a beat, cut on it: align every fourth or eighth photo to a bar so the sequence and the music agree. If there is no clear beat, hold each photo slightly longer than feels natural — the eye needs about a third of a second to settle after a change.

Common problems and what causes them

Audio and photos drift apartUsually a variable-length track, or a slideshow rendered in separate chunks. Render in one pass.
Music stops before the last photoThe track is shorter than the total duration. Loop the audio or remove photos.
Music cuts off mid-noteThe render stopped at the last photo instead of fading the track. Add a short fade-out.
Export fails on a long slideshowCanvas recording is memory-bound. Split into two files or lower the output resolution.
