Practical image to video guide

How to keep a face consistent in image to video.

Use a clear reference portrait, preserve identity details in the prompt, and limit the action to small movements a still face can plausibly make.

The practical consistency rule

Lock the camera, describe the person’s existing features as unchanged, and ask for only one quiet action such as a blink, a breath, or a slight gaze shift. Avoid a turn, close-up, dramatic expression change, or new lighting setup until the simple version stays stable.

Start with a reference that shows the face clearly

The source image is the identity reference for the clip. Use a sharp, well-lit portrait with the face visible and unobstructed. Sunglasses, heavy shadows, hair across the face, extreme angles, and tiny faces in a wide scene leave less reliable detail to preserve.

Better reference imageOne clearly visible face, even lighting, a natural expression, and enough space around the head and shoulders.
Harder reference imageA profile view, a very small face, intense shadows, multiple overlapping people, or strong motion blur.
Safer first actionA slow blink, gentle breathing, a small gaze movement, or hair moving in a light breeze.
Riskier first actionA full head turn, a large smile or shout, a camera push-in, or a scene change.

Write the prompt around what must not change

Put identity preservation before visual style. Name the visible details that should remain as they are, then request one restrained movement. The prompt is clearer when it excludes transformations instead of trying to describe every possible expression.

Face consistency promptKeep the same person, facial features, hairstyle, skin tone, clothing, background, and camera framing as the reference image. Locked camera. The person breathes naturally and blinks once while keeping the same calm expression. No face swap, no age change, no new accessories, no camera movement, no head turn, no new people, and no changes to the setting.

Test the smallest version first

  1. Choose one person. If the source contains a group, crop or select a frame where the intended subject is unmistakable.
  2. Freeze the viewpoint. Ask for a locked camera and keep the head angle close to the reference image.
  3. Request one small action. Test a blink or breath before combining gestures, walking, speech, or camera movement.
  4. Review individual frames. If identity drifts, remove the extra action and retest with steadier light and a simpler prompt.

Fix common consistency problems

The face changes shapeReduce the action, keep the camera fixed, and use a clearer front-facing source image.
The expression becomes exaggeratedDescribe the existing expression as unchanged and avoid emotional or dramatic style words.
Hair or clothing changesState that hairstyle and clothing remain unchanged, then remove wind, action, or scene-transition requests.
The image looks like a new personReturn to a stiller portrait and test one simple movement before adding effects or a new angle.

Build motion around identity, not the other way around

A short, stable clip is more useful than a busy sequence that loses the person in the reference frame. Once the simplest motion holds together, change one variable at a time: a slightly longer blink, a softer background movement, or a small shift in gaze.
